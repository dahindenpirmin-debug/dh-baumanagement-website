## Why a feasibility study?

A plot in an industrial zone, a fair price, a good location by the motorway – and the project can still fail: on the noise sensitivity level, on a watercourse corridor across the parcel, on missing power capacity, or on ground that demands half a million in piling. The feasibility study makes such points visible while a “no” still costs nothing.

## The seven building blocks

**1. Operational requirements.** Areas, heights, loads, processes, staff, lorry movements, shift work, growth scenario. Without this brief no plot can be assessed.

**2. Planning and building law.** Zone and permitted use, site coverage or floor area ratio, heights, setbacks from boundaries and roads, special land-use plans, easements in the land register, parking rules, townscape and heritage protection.

**3. Environment.** Noise (from the operation and its traffic), register of contaminated sites, groundwater and water protection, watercourse corridors, natural hazards, major-accident prevention, possibly an environmental impact assessment.

**4. Ground and topography.** Bearing capacity, groundwater level, slope, excavation and disposal class. A desk-based geological review often suffices for a first assessment.

**5. Access and utilities.** Access and swept paths, connection to the main road network, public transport for staff, capacity of power, water, sewerage, gas or district heating, and fibre.

**6. Massing study.** One or more development options to scale: building volume, floor areas, deliveries, parking, extension stage. It shows whether the space programme fits the plot – and what is left over.

**7. Cost, schedule, risks.** Rough cost estimate from benchmarks (±20–25%), time frame from design to operation, risk list with rating and measures.

## The result: a basis for decision

The outcome is not a thick dossier but a clear statement:

| Result | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Feasible | project can be realised without material reservations |
| Feasible with conditions | realisable if certain conditions are met (e.g. noise control, grid reinforcement, special design plan) |
| Not feasible | exclusion criterion present; examine alternatives |

It comes with a recommendation for next steps and a list of points that should be secured contractually before a purchase decision.

## Comparing locations

If several plots are available, a weighted scoring analysis is worthwhile: criteria such as land price, construction cost, access, availability of skilled staff, room for extension and time to readiness are weighted and scored. The method makes the decision traceable – especially towards a board or bank.

## Typical exclusion criteria

- Use not permitted in the zone, or noise limits cannot be met.
- Lorry access not approvable.
- Power capacity only available in several years.
- Contaminated site requiring remediation with unresolved cost allocation.
- Easements or building lines that prevent the required volume.
