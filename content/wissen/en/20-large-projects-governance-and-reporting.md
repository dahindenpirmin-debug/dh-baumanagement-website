## What makes large projects different

With size, it is not only the figures that grow but the number of parties, interfaces and decisions. A new production building for thirty million francs quickly involves twenty design firms, fifty contractors and a dozen internal stakeholder groups. Then there is duration: over five years, people, prices and sometimes corporate strategy change.

## Project organisation

| Level | Who | Task |
|---|---|---|
| Strategic | board, executive management, steering committee | goals, funding, phase approvals, fundamental decisions |
| Management | owner’s overall project manager, owner’s representative | steering, controlling, preparing decisions, reporting |
| Operational | lead consultant or design team, construction manager, contractors | design and construction |
| Users | operations, production, IT, facility management | requirements, acceptance, commissioning |

Two rules have proven themselves. First: **one** person leads the project on the owner’s side, with clear authority in francs. Second: users feed in requirements through a defined channel – not directly to the designers.

## Phase gates rather than a continuous run

Each phase ends with an approval request to the steering committee: project status, cost, schedule, risks, open points, and the request for the next phase with its funding tranche. Only the money needed until the next stopping point is released, and stopping or resizing remains possible.

## Integrated controlling

**Cost.** A budget structure covering construction, operating equipment, relocation, fees, contingencies and inflation. Monthly forecast of final cost, with approved, notified and expected changes tracked separately.

**Schedule.** Master programme with the owner’s milestones, with design and construction programmes beneath. Milestone trend analysis: a milestone that shifts from report to report is an early indicator, long before the completion date officially slips.

**Risks.** Risk register with probability, impact, owner and measure. The ten largest risks belong in every report.

**Changes.** Every project change follows the same process: request, assessment of cost, schedule and quality, decision at the appropriate level, update of budget and drawings.

**Quality.** Drawing approvals, sample approvals, inspection plans for critical components, integrated tests of building services.

## Reporting: short, regular, honest

A good monthly or quarterly report fits on two to four pages:

1. Overall assessment with traffic lights for cost, schedule, quality, risks.
2. Cost: budget, awards, forecast, contingency drawn.
3. Schedule: milestones planned and actual.
4. Top risks and measures.
5. Decisions required before the next report.

What matters is the honesty of the traffic light. A project that reports green for twelve months and then turns red did not have a cost problem but a reporting problem. Banks and investors therefore increasingly require independent construction controlling.

## Multi-year programmes

Owners delivering several projects over years – phased refurbishment, site development, expansion of a location – run them as a programme:

- **Rolling investment plan** over five to ten years, updated annually.
- **Standards** for recurring elements (building services, materials, contracts, documentation) so that not every project starts from zero.
- **Framework agreements** with designers and contractors for recurring services.
- **Knowledge retention:** lessons learned after each stage, uniform as-built documentation.
- **Alignment with operations:** construction stages, production planning and budget cycles interlock.

## Commissioning as a project of its own

For complex buildings, commissioning planning starts a year before occupation: tests of individual systems, integrated tests, authority inspections, training, trial operation, relocation. Treating it as an appendix to construction is paid for with delays to the start of production.
