## What distinguishes site development from a construction project

A construction project has a start, a budget and a handover. A site lives on while it is being rebuilt: tenants come and go, markets change, and so do regulations. What is needed is therefore not a finished picture but a structure that allows several futures.

## Phase 1: Analysis – what do we have?

- **Existing buildings:** condition, remaining life, areas, income and upkeep, building by building.
- **Law:** zone, density figures (in the Canton of Lucerne the site coverage ratio), heights, building lines, easements, ongoing revisions of local planning.
- **Environment:** register of contaminated sites, noise, watercourse corridors, natural hazards.
- **Infrastructure:** access roads, utilities, power capacity, energy supply.
- **Tenants and contracts:** terms, notice periods, options – they determine when which space becomes free.
- **Market:** regional demand for production, storage, office or residential space; achievable rents.

The outcome is a site balance sheet: what is worth keeping, what is reserve, what is risk?

## Phase 2: Use strategy – where do we want to go?

The owners define what the site should be in fifteen years: a production base for own use, a business park for SMEs, a mixed quarter or a combination. This includes target return, risk appetite and time horizon. Scenarios (“optimise existing”, “densify”, “transform”) are compared with rough figures.

## Phase 3: Master plan – the robust structure

The master plan defines what should no longer change and leaves open what may:

| Fix | Leave open |
|---|---|
| Access axes and entrances | Exact use per plot |
| Building plots and their maximum volume | Architecture of individual buildings |
| Open spaces, stormwater retention, service corridors | Sequence of later stages |
| Energy and utilities concept | Tenant fit-outs |

Site-wide energy supply – a heat network using waste heat, photovoltaics on all roofs and a self-consumption community – is now often a competitive advantage in letting.

## Phase 4: Secure planning rights

If standard rules suffice, stages can proceed directly with building applications. Otherwise a special development plan or rezoning is needed. These procedures take two to five years, require close cooperation with municipality and canton and trigger a value-capture levy where additional use is granted. Early dialogue with authorities and neighbours is not courtesy here but risk management.

## Phase 5: Economics and phasing

Each stage should be financeable on its own and leave the site in working order. A proven sequence:

1. **Quick wins in existing buildings:** let vacant space, renew roofs and fit PV, tidy up access.
2. **First new-build stage** on free or soon-to-be-free land – ideally with an anchor tenant.
3. **Decanting:** existing tenants move into the new building; old buildings become free for refurbishment or replacement.
4. **Further stages** according to demand.

The investment appraisal shows cost, income and funding need per stage, and the value of the site after each stage.

## Phase 6: Organisation

Site developments rarely fail on technology, often on organisation. They need an ownership capable of deciding, an overall project manager as permanent point of contact, and annual site reporting: status of master plan, letting, investments, risks, next steps.
